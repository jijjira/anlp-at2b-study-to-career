# Section 4.5 (rewrite): Application and user testing

## 4.5 Application and formative user testing

The Streamlit application turns the pipeline into a three-step workflow shown at the top of the interface (Figure 10): (1) choose a unit PDF or click "Try the sample outline", (2) pick a method, either "Fast: exact skill names" or "Deeper: also finds skills described in other words" (sidebar), and (3) read the results. The results page gives a one-sentence headline (the closest career and how many of its 30 most requested skills are covered), a ranked chart of the five careers, and an expandable panel per career with covered skills, the largest gaps, a plain-language explanation, a "what to learn next" table, the page and sentence behind each covered skill, and JSON and CSV downloads. A notice at the top states that a score shows how much of what employers ask for appears in the document; it is not proof of skill and not the chance of employment (Amershi et al., 2019).

On the sample outline, exact mode ranks Data Scientist (41.9%), Data Analyst (40.1%) and ML Engineer (39.0%), and the interface flags the top two as effectively tied because they are within two points. Hybrid mode raises ML Engineer to 41.7%, which places it second (Figure B1, Appendix B). This shows that semantic additions can change a ranking; it does not show that hybrid is more accurate, since about 64% of semantic-only additions were correct in the blinded audit, so they are marked "possibly covered".

**Test.** Three classmates completed the workflow on version 1 and rated five statements from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) (Table 7). Results and explanations were rated 5.00 by every participant, usefulness of skills and gaps 4.33, willingness to use the tool for exploring alignment 4.33, and ease of upload and analysis 3.33; the mean over all 15 ratings was 4.40. No participant reported a task-blocking problem. Only one participant (P1) explained a low ease rating, citing slow hybrid mode and the unclear difference between the two modes; P2 also rated ease at 3 without comment. P2 and P3 commented on what the tool does not measure (experience and education; the job market), which is a limit of the method and not a usability finding.

**Changes.** Each change maps to a comment: plain-language method names with a one-line explanation, a spinner message that the first hybrid run loads a model, and a "how to read the results" panel (P1); a notice that experience and education are not measured (P2); a notice that a score is not a chance of employment (P3). A sample-outline button (for first-time users, in response to the ease rating), a tie warning and "possibly covered" marking were added in the same revision. These changes have not been re-tested, so their effect on ease of use is unknown.

**How far this can be trusted.** The test is formative: three classmates, one session, self-reported ratings, no task-completion or time measures, and nine of the fifteen ratings at the top of the scale. It can identify interface and interpretation problems early (Nielsen, 1994), but it cannot estimate usability for students in general, and ratings of "clarity" cannot show that users read the percentage correctly. A stronger evaluation would re-test the revised interface with at least five students from outside the project on a fixed task list (find the closest career, name two gaps, explain what 41.9% means), record completion, time and errors, and add a standard questionnaire such as the System Usability Scale.

Table 7 is unchanged. The measure "Would use to explore alignment" corresponds to Q5 in the data file (`user_testing_results.csv`).
