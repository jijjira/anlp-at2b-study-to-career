## X.1 Approach and justification
We extract skills from job advertisements and (with the identical function) from student study material using a **dictionary-driven spaCy `PhraseMatcher`** over a 132-skill, alias-normalised taxonomy (Step 2). We chose a transparent matcher as the *baseline* for three reasons: (i) every output is traceable to an alias and a text span, which matters when a student is told they "lack" a skill; (ii) it needs no labelled training data, which does not exist for our domain; and (iii) it yields a strong, cheap reference against which the semantic (Sentence-Transformer) method [Member 4] can be judged. Case-insensitive matching is used for ordinary aliases and case-sensitive matching for the language "R"; text and aliases share one preprocessing step (Unicode/dash/slash/punctuation normalisation) so that "C++,Go" or "Node.js/TypeScript" tokenise identically on both sides.

## X.1b Student-side pipeline (PDF study material)
Uploaded PDF study materials are read page by page with **PyMuPDF** and cleaned before matching: running headers/footers (lines repeated on at least half of the pages) and page numbers are removed, words split by end-of-line hyphenation are rejoined, soft line wraps are merged so that phrases such as "machine learning" are not split across lines, and text is split into one sentence per line so the ambiguity guard works per sentence. The cleaned text is then passed to the **same PhraseMatcher, dictionary, reviewed aliases and normalisation** that are used on job advertisements, which keeps both sides of the comparison consistent. Output is a set of canonical skills with mention counts, page numbers and an evidence sentence, presented to users as "skills covered by your study materials" and never as mastery. Limitations: scanned PDFs without a text layer are not supported (no OCR), multi-column slides can interleave text, and testing so far used a synthetic unit outline and unit tests, not yet real student PDFs.

## X.2 Extensions beyond the baseline ("Baseline++")
1. **Requirement-aware extraction.** Each ad is segmented into sections (required, duty, preferred, company/benefits) using a header lexicon plus line-level cues; every skill mention inherits a strength. Skills that occur only in company text ("founded by the creators of Apache Spark") are treated as non-demand. Strengths are converted to demand weights (1 / 0.75 / 0.5 / 0.5 / 0), producing **demand-weighted career profiles** instead of raw mention counts.
2. **Context guards.** URLs/e-mails are removed; ambiguous lowercase aliases ("spark", "hive", "go") count only when the line contains another known skill.
3. **Human-in-the-loop dictionary discovery.** Unknown tokens appearing in lists beside ≥2 known skills were mined from development ads only, ranked, and reviewed manually (336 candidates → 45 accepted aliases for 36 skills). Plural/singular variants were added for multi-word aliases.
4. **Marginal-gain signal.** Career fit = demand-weighted share of a career's top-30 skills covered; the demand weight of each missing skill is its marginal gain, a transparent ranking for the "what to learn next" view [Member 5].

## X.2b Alignment layer: coverage strength, uncertainty and curriculum view
Three additions turn skill lists into an alignment analysis. (1) **Coverage strength on the study-material side:** a skill found under a learning-outcomes or assessment heading, or in a sentence about assessment, counts as *assessed* (weight 1.0); a skill in a weekly schedule or topic list as *taught* (0.75); a skill only in an overview or prerequisites as *mentioned* (0.5). This mirrors the required/preferred weighting on the job side. (2) **Uncertainty:** career fit is the demand-weighted share of a career's top-30 skills that the material covers, and a 95% bootstrap range is obtained by resampling each career's advertisements 500 times, so careers with few ads (Data Analyst, n = 27) show visibly wider ranges and close rankings are not over-interpreted. (3) **Curriculum view:** several units are merged by taking the strongest coverage of each skill, and the result is shown as a skills-by-careers heatmap with the uncovered high-demand skills ranked by marginal gain. The weights are hyper-parameters, the coverage tagger is a heading-and-cue heuristic, and the bootstrap captures sampling noise in the ads only, not bias in where the ads came from. The demonstration uses four synthetic unit outlines, so no finding about a real degree is claimed until real outlines are analysed.

## X.2c Sector lens
Skill demand is usually reported per job title, but employers differ as well. We assigned each of the 106 employers to one of seven sectors by hand (company-to-sector table supplied for group review; borderline cases listed) and treated a sector as a main sector only with at least 60 ads from at least 8 companies: AI labs and AI-native products (173 ads), Consumer, social and marketplaces (144), Cloud, data and developer infrastructure (107), Enterprise and B2B software (101), Autonomy, defense and physical-world tech (72) and Fintech and trading (69). Health and wellness tech (22 ads, 7 companies) is reported as exploratory only. Because ads from one company are not independent, all intervals are **company-clustered bootstraps** and the sector test is a **company-level permutation test**.
Findings: sector demand profiles differ more than chance (permutation p = 0.004), but much of this reflects career mix; after subtracting each ad's career average the evidence is borderline (p = 0.06), so we claim that sector differences exist and are largely, not necessarily entirely, explained by what each sector hires for. Four skills form a universal core (demand weight at least 0.20 in all six sectors): Python, Machine Learning, Large Language Models and the generic term Artificial Intelligence. Sector-distinctive skills with company-clustered intervals that exclude zero include Statistics (+0.31, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.45), Machine Learning and Causal Inference in Consumer, AI Agents, Large Language Models and Observability in Enterprise software, Fine-tuning and Distributed Systems in Infrastructure, Computer Vision and Kubernetes in Autonomy and defense (Computer Vision interval 0.02 to 0.90, very wide because two companies supply most ads), and SQL in Fintech. AI labs have no robust distinctive skill and behave like the market default. The same demo curriculum fits sectors differently (fit 0.48 for Consumer to 0.25 for Infrastructure), which shows why a curriculum should be judged per sector; this uses synthetic units, so it demonstrates the method and is not a finding about a real degree.
Limitations: manual sector labels, career-mix confounding, concentration in a few large tech employers, mostly 2026 ads, and a thin health sample.

## X.2d Case study: Australian units against the U.S. benchmark
To answer the project question directly, the PDF pipeline was run on 20 public unit documents saved as PDFs on 4 Oct 2026: UTS 10, USYD 5 and UNSW 5, using the newest offering found for each (listed with URLs in `data/curriculum_manifest.csv` and `docs/references_and_data_sources.md`). Seven (the "core" group) are machine-learning units collected first; thirteen were added to cover databases, big-data engineering, statistics, visualisation, data-science foundations, NLP and enterprise software. The documents cover 39 distinct skills. Taking the best coverage per skill across all 20 units, the share of each career's top-30 demanded skills that is covered is highest for Data Scientist (0.65, 95% range 0.63 to 0.69), then Data Engineer (0.59), ML Engineer (0.58) and Data Analyst (0.58), and lowest for AI Engineer (0.33). This is a catalogue-level figure (a student would have to combine units from three universities), not any one student's path.

Selection mattered most. With only the 7 core ML units, Data Engineer fit was 0.13 and SQL, Spark and Snowflake were gaps; adding the added units raises it to 0.59 and closes those gaps, so an ML-only sample would have overstated the mismatch. Gaps that remain across all 20 units are, for Data Scientist, Causal Inference, Dashboarding, A/B Testing, Experiment Design and Forecasting; for ML Engineer, PyTorch, Generative AI, Observability and Kubernetes; for Data Engineer, dbt, ETL and the cloud platforms (AWS, GCP, Azure); and for AI Engineer, APIs, agents and RAG. Fit by employer sector ranges from 0.66 for consumer and marketplace employers to 0.44 for autonomy and physical-world employers, but the company-clustered intervals overlap, so only that top-versus-bottom contrast is suggestive. Career rankings at the top and bottom are unchanged under flat weights.

This is a case study of 20 selected data/AI units, not a claim about whole degrees or all Australian higher education. Handbook PDFs are shorter than full outlines (UTS shows only the description and learning outcomes, so UTS coverage is a lower bound), the extractor was validated on job ads rather than unit documents, and three false matches were removed by manual review with reasons recorded (for example server clustering read as ML clustering). Exact-phrase matching also misses concepts described but not named: the UNSW COMP9311 Database Systems handbook text never says "SQL" and yields no skills, which is the gap the semantic method (Section Y) targets.

**What the real PDFs exposed.** Running the reader on real browser print-outs found four bugs, all fixed and regression-tested: print headers padded with long runs of spaces were not recognised as running headers (title words were credited to every page), indented wrapped lines were not rejoined, requisite lists, excluded courses, field-of-education codes and "further study" sentences were credited as coverage, and table cells and side columns were glued into one line (which inflated "assessed" levels and credited assessment-policy tables such as "AI allowed" as skills). "Topics covered include ..." sentences now count as taught.

## X.3 Experimental design
A stratified **held-out set of 30 ads** (6 per career; seed 36118) was drawn before tuning; all discovery and rule tuning excluded these ads. Skills were annotated blind to system output into four tiers: explicit-required (R), explicit-preferred (P), implicit/paraphrased (I) and out-of-dictionary (OTHER). Systems S0-S5 add one component at a time. We report micro precision/recall/F1 on R∪P and **paired-bootstrap** (2,000 resamples over ads) confidence intervals for differences.

## X.4 Results
| System | P | R | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 PhraseMatcher baseline | .902 | .844 | .872 |
| S3 + context guard & section filter | .925 | .836 | .878 |
| S4 + reviewed aliases | .904 | .976 | .938 |
| S5 + plural/singular variants | .904 | .984 | .943 |

* S0→S5: ΔF1 = +0.071, 95% CI [0.039, 0.104]. The significant gain comes from **dictionary discovery** (S3→S4: +0.060, CI [0.027, 0.095]); the dictionary, not the matcher, was the bottleneck.
* The section/ambiguity filter does **not** significantly change F1 (+0.006, CI [−0.006, 0.021]); it removes 6 false positives at the cost of 2 true positives. Its value is the demand-strength signal. Demand-weighted rankings are insensitive to the chosen weights (Spearman ρ ≥ 0.99 across four weight sets) yet differ from raw frequency (ρ = 0.65-0.91 by career), e.g. for AI Engineer, RAG and AI Agents enter the top 15 while GCP and Fine-tuning drop out.
* Required-vs-preferred tagging reaches 87.9% accuracy on true positives (n = 247) versus 84.6% for the trivial "all required" rule; we treat it as a soft weight, not a classifier.
* **Ceiling of a dictionary method:** 27% of the skills a human reads in the 30 ads are implicit (12.6%) or outside the dictionary (14.3%). This quantifies the need for the semantic layer and is its evaluation target [Member 4].
* Without the vague label "Artificial Intelligence", S0→S5 F1 is .901→.95, so conclusions do not hinge on that label.

## X.5 Challenges
Ambiguous short tokens (Go, R, Spark, Ray), ads that mix duties, requirements and company boilerplate under inconsistent headers, abbreviation/plural variation, and emerging terms (MCP, agents) absent from a static taxonomy. A bug where requirement bullets starting with "Experience with…" were parsed as headers was found through error analysis and fixed.

## X.6 Limitations
* Gold labels come from **one annotator** (an AI model) working blind to system output; scores are silver-standard and n = 30 ads, so intervals are wide. Independent human re-annotation of a subset (and Cohen's κ) is the first thing we would add.
* One header/cue rule was informed by an ad in the evaluation set, a small source of optimism.
* Remaining false positives: degree lists ("degree in Math, Statistics"), mission statements outside recognised headers, and comparison mentions ("similar to Airflow"). Degree- and comparison-cues are proposed, not implemented.
* Data are US-only; the Australian collection (14 ads) is too small for evaluation.
* Sentence-Transformer results are reported by [Member 4]; none are claimed here.

## X.7 Positioning against existing systems
Commercial curriculum-to-skills tools such as **Lightcast SkillsMatch** map course content to a very large proprietary skills library, but are closed, not tied to a specific student's materials, and not inspectable. Research systems learn skill extraction with encoders (**Decorte et al., 2025**, ConTeXT-match / JobBERT-V2 trained on a large synthetic skill-labelled corpus) or supervised NER (**Fareri et al., 2021**, SkillNER, F1 ≈ 72.6% for soft skills); these need training corpora and are less explainable. Our contribution is a **lightweight, auditable, requirement-aware baseline** evaluated on held-out ads with explicit ablations, plus a quantified account of what such a baseline cannot see, which motivates our hybrid design.
